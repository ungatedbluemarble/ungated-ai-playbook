The Ungated AI Playbook · November 2026

Most enterprises are doing AI the expensive way. It doesn't have to be like this.

I've spent the last few years building AI programs inside real businesses, across every department I could get a seat with. What I've learned is that the right way to do this isn't secret, and it isn't something you need to buy. So I'm writing it down, in the open, for anyone who wants to use it.

Where most companies are right now

Walk into most enterprises today and you'll find the same pattern. Everything runs on the most powerful frontier model available, for every task, whether it needs it or not. Nobody benchmarked the alternatives. Nobody wrote down what a good result looks like. The bill arrives, it's bigger than anyone expected, and the plan is to cross their fingers and hope the value shows up.

The numbers back this up. In a 2026 survey of 700 engineering leaders, companies estimated they waste about a quarter of what they spend on AI. Half had no clear owner for those costs, and only about one in four had a solid way to measure what the spending was actually worth.

The second pattern worries me more. Companies that arrived late to AI are trying to buy their way back to the front. And they're increasingly buying from startups that want to do the company's own core work for them. Chip companies are being pitched AI that designs chips. Think about that. The people who know silicon best are being asked to rent that knowledge back from someone else.

If AI is going to change how your core work gets done, your own people need to be the ones who know how to do it.

None of this is a technology problem. It's a maturity problem, and it's fixable.

Before you buy anything

The industry expects you to sign another contract. Before you do, answer a few honest questions.

A skunkworks project is a small, protected team given room to experiment. Run one inside your engineering group and it will surface the AI uses that fit your work faster than any vendor pitch.

If you do bring in outside help, it should teach, set things up, and leave. Anything else is just a new dependency.

The standard, step by step

This is the order I work in. None of it requires a particular vendor, and all of it works on Claude, Copilot, ChatGPT, Gemini, or whatever your contracts already say you use.

Start with what you already own

Some companies choose their AI tools freely. Others inherit them through contracts they already hold. Either way, the first job is to learn that platform deeply and find what it does best for this particular business. Wishing for a different tool is not a strategy.

Ask everyone the same questions

The real problems live with department leaders, power users, and the experts who know a process inside out. I sit with them and ask the same core questions every time, so differences come from their answers and not from how I asked. When two leaders describe the same process differently, that disagreement is usually the most useful thing I learn all week.

Put every team at one table

Infrastructure, engineering, R&D, security and AI specialists all think differently, and each one sees a risk the others miss. The decisions that stall most programs, like whether a tool runs on your own systems or in the cloud, or which automations are worth building, get settled faster when they're all in the room.

Prepare before you prompt

This applies to every team and every skill level, even when engineers find it tedious. Plan the work. Clean the data you'll feed the model so it only holds what the task needs. Write down what a good result looks like before you see any output. Then test several models against that, instead of trusting the first one that looks right.

Use the right model for each stage

Models are tools for stages of work, not one purchase. Here's how a solution should move from idea to something you run every day.

In Claude terms, that might mean exploring with the top models, letting Sonnet plan over a few sessions, handing the plan to Opus to build, and saving Fable for short, demanding bursts. Every vendor has the same tiers under different names. And once something is proven, reliable and repeatable, stop paying frontier prices for it. Move it to a local model you control, tested the same way.

Cost gets judged by what gets finished, with failed attempts counted. A cheap model that needs four tries isn't cheap. When a team can show what AI delivered for what it cost, the conversation with leadership changes from "are we spending too much?" to "where do we invest next?"

Build the rules into the tools

You can't let people build with AI and also stop them from building. Review queues where someone approves every request fall apart at scale and quietly kill adoption. The better design puts the guardrails inside the tools people use, so the safe path is the easy one, and grounds each rule in requirements your business is actually bound by.

This comes from my years in messaging compliance. A message to someone who opted out shouldn't be blocked at the last second; the system shouldn't be able to draft it at all. The same thinking works everywhere: apply the rules before output, make every claim show its evidence, and when information is missing, stop and ask instead of guessing. Someone new to AI who gets one confident, wrong answer rarely comes back.

Argue with ideas before you invest in them

I test new ideas by running them past several AI perspectives at once and letting them argue until the weak spots show. It's a cheap way to stress an idea before it costs a team real time, and it's how most of what's on this page took shape.

Grow your own people

Lasting adoption comes from people who understand what they're doing. That means workshops shaped around each department, champions inside every team who can teach the people next to them, and tools that teach as they go. The goal is a company that keeps getting better at this long after any consultant has left.
